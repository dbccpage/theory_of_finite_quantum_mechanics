## Post 3 — what Many Worlds says (490 characters)

Many Worlds, the simple version. The equations of quantum mechanics never pick an outcome. Record which slit the electron took and the math gives one combined state with two terms: (went left, detector says left) + (went right, detector says right). Many Worlds says: take that at face value. Both terms keep evolving, each with its own detector reading and its own you reading it. They stop interfering because their records differ. No collapse, nothing thrown away. That's the whole idea.

## Post 4 — why it attracts, what it costs (451 characters)

Why smart people like it: it adds no extra rule. "Collapse" is a second law nobody has ever caught in the act; Many Worlds drops it and keeps only the Schrödinger equation. What it costs: if every outcome happens, what does a 70% chance mean? Which "you" sees the 30%? And the line between "system" and "surroundings" that fixes where branches split is something we choose, not something the equation hands us. These are open problems, not knockdowns.

## Post 5 — how I look at it (447 characters)

How I look at it. A formula's terms are bookkeeping until some measurement tells them apart. "The branches are separate worlds" and "the branches are alternatives in one world" predict the same result in every experiment anyone has designed, so experiment can't choose between them. My rule: add nothing to the math that no measurement could ever notice. That's not a disproof of Many Worlds. It's a refusal to promote a description into a census.

## Post 6 — what would settle it (485 characters)

So what would settle it? Many Worlds says interference never really dies; it hides in records too spread out to recombine. Rival theories say big enough objects lose interference on their own, and that is being tested right now with heavier and heavier molecules. If those tests find spontaneous loss, unitary quantum mechanics, Many Worlds included, is wrong. If they never do, both stories survive and the choice stays philosophical. Either way the experiment, not the vote, decides.
