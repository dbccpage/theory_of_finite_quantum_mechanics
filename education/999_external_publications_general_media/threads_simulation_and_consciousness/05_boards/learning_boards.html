Does the universe render?

A post in a thread about the double slit said:

“It's just like a video game, the universe does not render what is offscreen.”

It linked a 2024 paper. These boards check the sentence and the paper the same way, one move per board. Each move works on any claim.

Board 1 of 6 · sort before you argue

What is on the record?

Start with an old question. A tree falls in a forest with nobody there. Did it make a sound?

  1. WitnessedTwo records. In the first, two upright strokes. In the second, one upright stroke and one flat one.
  2. SuppliedThat the flat stroke is one of the upright ones. The records do not label them.
  3. SuppliedA fall between the two records. Nothing between them was recorded.
  4. SuppliedA sound. That takes a model of air, and no microphone left a record.
  5. DerivedSo “did it make a sound?” is a question about the supplied parts. The records alone cannot answer it.

The same sort, applied to the post:

WordTagWhy
renderOpenNo rule says what rendering is, or how to measure it.
what is offscreenSuppliedA screen, and a place to look from.
our consciousnessOpenFrom the same post: “it's our consciousness.” Named, with no carrier, record or rule.
just like a video gameSuppliedAn analogy. It shows the claim can be pictured. It does not show the claim holds.

SoNo part of the sentence points to a record of rendering yet. That does not make it false. It means nothing in it has been checked. The paper it linked is checked on board 5.

Board 2 of 6 · split the word

Which job does the universe skip?

In a game, “render” covers three separate jobs. A game can skip any of them where the player is not looking.

  1. CitedGames skip these jobs separately. Minecraft, for one, stops updating chunks far from the player, and builds chunks nobody has visited from the world seed on arrival, the same way every time for a given seed and game version.
  2. DerivedNone of the three jobs follows from another. The table has a case of each: run and kept but not drawn, and kept but not run.
  3. SuppliedWhich job the universe skips. The post does not say, and each choice is a different claim.
  4. DerivedEach reading either predicts some record that would come out differently, or predicts the same records as a world that does every job.
  5. Witnessed“Not run” predicts that things stop changing while nobody looks. The records say otherwise. A clock left in an empty room keeps time. Ice in a closed box melts. Rock layers that formed with nobody watching carry a long history.
  6. Derived“Not drawn”, and “skipped, then caught up in full when someone looks”, predict the same records as a world that does every job. No record can favor them over the ordinary account. Board 4 shows why.

SoName the job. Then name what you would see that you would not see otherwise. If the answer is “nothing”, the claim adds a story and no test.

Your turn

Someone says the universe skips the “run” job, then catches up at once whenever you look. What experiment would test that?

Show an answer

None that uses records. Every record would come out the same as if it had run all along, because the catch-up was built to erase the difference. That protects the claim from every test. For the same reason, no test can ever support it.

When a reading can be tested

testable  ⟺  some record r has P(r∣skips)≠P(r∣does every job)\text{testable} \iff \text{some record } r \text{ has } P(r \mid \text{skips}) \neq P(r \mid \text{does every job})

“Not run” gives a stopped clock a high chance. The ordinary account gives it almost none. So the clock is a test, and every clock that kept time counts against “not run”.

caught up in full:P(r∣skips)=P(r∣does every job)  for every r\text{caught up in full:}\quad P(r \mid \text{skips}) = P(r \mid \text{does every job}) \ \text{ for every } r

A full catch-up makes the two chances equal for every record by construction, so no record can tell them apart.

Board 3 of 6 · count the channels

Can't tell on this channel, or can't tell at all?

Take two worlds. World A has one extra thing in a place nobody looks. World B does not.

  1. SuppliedA and B differ only where nobody looks.
  2. SuppliedRead “nobody looks there” as: every channel in use reports A and B the same way. This is our reading of the post's word.
  3. DerivedSo any rule that decides from those reports alone gives A and B the same verdict.
  4. DerivedKeep three claims apart. (a) These channels cannot tell A from B. (b) No channel could. (c) A and B are the same world. Step 3 proves (a) and nothing more. A new channel, like the phone in the fridge, can split A from B, so (a) does not give (b). Going from (b) to (c) takes an extra rule: worlds that no channel can split are the same. That rule is supplied.
  5. DerivedWhen there are more states than reports, two states must share a report. So anything with fewer possible reports than the states it looks at has blind spots. With at least as many reports as states, every state can get its own. Blind spots are ordinary. They are not a sign of a simulation.
  6. CitedThe post set aside the question of a human observer versus a measurement device. That is the question the double slit can settle. When any device records which slit, the interference fringes go away. Whether a person ever reads the record makes no difference.

SoTo make the claim checkable, name a channel on which A and B would differ, and say what it would show.

Your turn

A thermometer shows one decimal place. It reads 20.0 for both 20.01° and 20.04°. Which of (a), (b) and (c) hold?

Show an answer

(a) holds: this thermometer cannot tell them apart. (b) fails: a finer thermometer can. (c) fails: they are different temperatures.

Blind spots, written out

Let q be the report a channel gives for a state. If two states get the same report, every rule J that sees only the report gives them the same verdict:

q(a)=q(b)  ⟹  J(q(a))=J(q(b))for every rule J that reads only qq(a) = q(b) \;\Longrightarrow\; J\big(q(a)\big) = J\big(q(b)\big) \quad \text{for every rule } J \text{ that reads only } q

With S the set of states and P the set of reports, more states than reports forces a shared report. This is the pigeonhole principle:

∣S∣>∣P∣  ⟹  there are s≠s′ with q(s)=q(s′)|S| > |P| \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{there are } s \neq s' \text{ with } q(s) = q(s')

And the condition matters. With enough reports, no blind spot is forced:

∣S∣≤∣P∣  ⟹  some q gives every state its own report|S| \le |P| \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{some } q \text{ gives every state its own report}

Board 4 of 6 · compare chances, not stories

Does matching the data make it evidence?

The first reply in the thread said: if a simulation reproduces quantum mechanics exactly, the double-slit data cannot favor it. Here is what that rests on.

  1. DerivedMeasure evidence by a ratio: the chance of the data under one story, divided by its chance under the other. This is the Bayes factor. Above 1 favors the first story, below 1 the second, and exactly 1 favors neither.
  2. DerivedAllowing the same results is not enough. Story A gives heads 9 times in 10, story B 5 times in 10. Both allow heads. One head has a chance of 0.9 against 0.5, a factor of 1.8.
  3. DerivedEqual chances one reading at a time are not enough either. Take two separate fair coins, or one fair coin read twice. Each reading is heads half the time in both. Heads-then-heads has a chance of 1/4 against 1/2, a factor of 2. Compare the chance of the whole record, not its pieces.
  4. DerivedWhen two stories give every whole record the same chance, and the record has a chance above zero, the factor is exactly 1. That is the case the first reply named.
  5. SuppliedThe post's simulation gives no chances at all, so its factor cannot be computed yet.

SoA story built to match the ordinary account on every record can never be favored over it by a record, and never counted against by one either. To become evidence, it has to risk a different number somewhere.

Your turn

Story X: the die is fair. Story Y: the die is fair, and someone unseen watches every roll. You roll a 4. What is the factor?

Show an answer

1. Both stories give a 4 a chance of 1/6. The roll says nothing for or against the watcher. Only a record whose chance the watcher changes could.

The Bayes factor, with its conditions

K=P(D∣H1)P(D∣H2)K = \frac{P(D \mid H_1)}{P(D \mid H_2)}

Same reports allowed, different chances:

P(heads∣A)=910,P(heads∣B)=510,K=1.8P(\text{heads} \mid A) = \tfrac{9}{10}, \quad P(\text{heads} \mid B) = \tfrac{5}{10}, \quad K = 1.8

Same chance for each reading, different chance for the record:

P(HH∣two coins)=14,P(HH∣one coin read twice)=12,K=2P(\text{HH} \mid \text{two coins}) = \tfrac14, \quad P(\text{HH} \mid \text{one coin read twice}) = \tfrac12, \quad K = 2

Multiplying the chances of single readings gives the chance of the record only under independence. The coin read twice is the case where it fails:

P(d1,…,dn∣H)=∏i=1nP(di∣H)only when the draws are independentP(d_1, \dots, d_n \mid H) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(d_i \mid H) \quad \text{only when the draws are independent}

The first reply's case, with the condition it needs. The denominator must be above zero, or the ratio is not defined:

P(D∣H1)=P(D∣H2)>0  ⟹  K=1P(D \mid H_1) = P(D \mid H_2) > 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; K = 1

Board 6 of 6 · check it on its own terms

Where does the paper's math hold?

A fair test uses the paper's own equations and inputs. Give credit where they hold, name the exact step where they break, and keep the rest open.

  • HoldsDerivedThe capacity arithmetic. From the paper's own inputs, 2.6 petabytes without its intermediate rounding (the paper says 2.5), against 31 terabytes for one bit per synapse (the paper says 30). The inputs are supplied: a 2 mm cortex treated as one uniform optical medium, 500 nm light, and a storage bound taken from another paper. Almost all of the 2.6 petabytes comes from counting the 2 mm thickness as storage. A hologram on the surface alone, over the same area, holds about 0.5 terabytes, less than the synapse estimate.
  • BreaksDerivedThe light pulse in Appendix A. The paper fixes its fields with two conditions. Put into its own equation (A7), they force the charge density to stay constant. The pulse needs that density to oscillate. So the conditions used to derive the pulse rule the pulse out. The paper calls the two conditions the Coulomb gauge, which fixes only the second and lets the scalar potential follow the charge. Done that way, the pulse comes back, at the price of an extra Coulomb term the paper would have to show is small.
  • BreaksDerivedNumericalThe simulations in Appendix C. Gauss's law ties the electric field to the charge at every moment. The paper's Maxwell terms keep it. Three added terms break it: a mass term, a damping term, and the coupling between layers. In a rerun of the paper's simulation, the error in Gauss's law is 1.1 to 2.3 times the size of the change in charge it should match.
  • Does not followDerivedSupplied“No risk of thermal degradation.” The paper compares a 2.4 eV photon with 20 meV, the least heat released in erasing one bit at body temperature, under conditions given on the back page. That bound is about erasing, and the photon's energy is not a barrier. It says nothing about how long a stored pattern lasts. A record held by one energy barrier needs about 1.2 eV to last a year at body temperature if the barrier is tested every picosecond, or 0.46 eV if every second. That rate is supplied, and the paper gives no barrier.
  • AssumedWitnessedThe hologram. The paper supposes the stored pattern is the interference of a reference wave and an object wave. The object wave has no equation in the model. So the model shows how a hologram would be read once one is written.
  • OpenOpenStill unsettled. The size of the input (the paper says about 3, and our recursion of its formulas gives 2.13). The near-field term dropped in Appendix A. The value of the mass scale m. A comparison of our rerun with the authors' own code. A record spread over many molecules, which could have a larger barrier than one molecule.

SoThe arithmetic holds. Two derivations break under the paper's own equations. The central step, writing the hologram, is assumed. None of it is about what the universe renders.

Appendix A: the two conditions freeze the charge

A0=0  ⟹  ∇⋅E=− ∂t(∇⋅A)A^0 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \nabla\cdot\mathbf E = -\,\partial_t(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A)
(A7):∇⋅E=J0=−e ∣φ∣2\text{(A7):}\quad \nabla\cdot\mathbf E = J_0 = -e\,\lvert\varphi\rvert^2
∇⋅A=0  ⟹  ∂t∣φ∣2=0\nabla\cdot\mathbf A = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \partial_t \lvert\varphi\rvert^2 = 0

The paper sets the time-independent part of (A7) to zero. With it kept, the density can only be static. The pulse comes from the part of the density that oscillates at the transition frequency, so either way there is no pulse. The repair keeps ∇·A = 0 and lets the scalar potential solve Poisson's equation:

∇2A0=−J0\nabla^2 A^0 = -J_0

Then (A7) no longer forces the density to stay fixed, and the pulse returns. The new Coulomb term is of order e²N/L, and the paper would have to show it is negligible.

Appendix C: what breaks Gauss's law

∂t(∇⋅E−J0)=−M2 ∇⋅A  −  γ ∂t∇⋅A  +  v ∇⋅A(n±1)\partial_t\big(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E - J^0\big) = -M^2\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf A \;-\; \gamma\,\partial_t \nabla\cdot\mathbf A \;+\; v\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf A^{(n\pm1)}

Everything else cancels exactly. The three terms on the right are the mass term M², the damping γ and the layer coupling v. This uses e²|φ|²/m, as in (A71) and (A82). The e|φ|²/m printed in (A76) and (A78) would break Gauss's law even without the added terms, so it is probably a typo. The rerun matches the paper's density dips to within about 10%, so it is very likely the same simulation. Nobody has yet compared it with the authors' code.

Here τ₀ is how often the barrier is tested. It is an input. The formula assumes one barrier. A photon's energy does not enter: once the photon is absorbed, what holds the pattern in place is the barrier, and the paper names none.

What the erasure bound says

kBTln⁡2∣310 K=18.5 meV,hc500 nm=2.48 eVk_B T \ln 2 \big|_{310\,\mathrm K} = 18.5\ \mathrm{meV}, \qquad \frac{hc}{500\ \mathrm{nm}} = 2.48\ \mathrm{eV}

kBT ln 2 is the least heat released when one bit is erased into surroundings at temperature T, under three conditions: the bit's value is unknown, it carries a full bit of uncertainty, and nothing kept is correlated with it. If an identical copy is kept, the bit can be reset reversibly at no such cost. The bound is about erasing, and says nothing about retention. The paper rounds 18.5 meV to 20 meV and 2.48 eV to 2.4 eV.

The method behind every board

How to take a claim apart

  1. State the claim in one sentence, in the speaker's own words.
  2. Sort its parts: witnessed, derived, numerical, cited, supplied, open.
  3. Try to break each part. Look for a counterexample, or a check that fails.
  4. Repair what broke, and write down what changed and why.
  5. Run the check again on the repair.
  6. List what is still owed.

We ran this on our own replies too, and found our own mistakes. One was a wrong fact about how Minecraft stores its world. Another was a quote from the paper read as being about the unobserved world, when it is about the observed one. Both are fixed above.

RuleSeveral AI reviewers checked this work, and their agreement counts for nothing. What counts is a check anyone can run again: a quote you can find, a computation, or a machine-checked proof.

Every step on these boards has such a check behind it. The full record lists each one, with the paper's equations, the computations and the proofs.
