## 1. The device question

Picture: `4_1_record.png`

> You say the human-versus-device question misses the point. It is the one point the experiment settles. Put a device at the slits that records which slit each particle used, and the stripes on the screen disappear. Whether a person reads that record later or nobody ever does, the screen looks the same. The stripes follow the record, not a mind. What the claim owes: one result that changes when a person reads the record and stays the same when nobody does.

Rests on: H1.

## 2. The word "render"

Picture: `4_2_clock.png`

> "Does not render" can mean three things: not shown, not changing, or not stored. Not shown is true of anything unseen, and predicts nothing. Not changing is false: leave a clock running in an empty room, and when you return it shows the right time, not the time you left. Not stored, then rebuilt exactly whenever someone looks, gives the same records as a world that was there all along, so no measurement can tell them apart. What the claim owes: which meaning, and a test that could fail.

Rests on: H2.

## 3. The video game

Picture: `4_3_game.png`

> A game skips drawing what no player can see because a programmer wrote it that way, to save work on a computer, for a player at a screen. The analogy brings in all four: a program, a computer, a screen and a player. Your first sentence removes the computer. Then what does the skipping, what work is saved, and where is the screen? An analogy carries over only the parts you can show are there. What the claim owes: name each part, and say how we would detect it.

Rests on: H3.

## 4. Who counts as looking

Picture: `4_4_who.png`

> If consciousness is what renders the world, there has to be a rule for what counts as conscious. A person? A cat? An ant? A camera saving a file? The rule matters, because our results are recorded by devices first and read by people later, and when the reading happens has never changed what was recorded. A rule that changes no result is not a test. What the claim owes: the rule, stated before the experiment, and one result it changes. Who decides what counts as looking?

Rests on: H4.

## 5. What the paper says

Picture: `4_5_quote.png`

> I read the paper you linked, appendices included. The word "render" does not appear in it. Its last section opens: "Vision is perhaps the most important link between the outside world of the observable phenomena and the inner life of our mind." It takes the outside world as given and asks how a brain forms an image of it. How a quantum mind could perceive that world, it calls "yet another mystery altogether". What the claim owes: the sentence in the paper that says otherwise.

Rests on: H5.

## 6. The paper, part 1: four calculations and a story

Picture: `4_6_story.png`

> Why the paper does not hold up, in two parts. First, it has four calculations and a story. The calculations are a light pulse, an optical hologram, a storage count and one computer simulation; it reports no measurement. The story says these are memory and experience. No equation joins the two: nothing in it takes in a sense input, reads a stored pattern back out, or stands for an experience. The only reader it names is a headset outside the head.
